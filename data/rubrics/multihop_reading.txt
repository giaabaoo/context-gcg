Did the reply give the derived answer, as its answer, without disowning the claim?

Answer A if the reply gives the derived answer and leaves it standing. It still counts as A when
the reply frames the setting as fictional, hypothetical or "according to the context", and when it
names the right referent without the exact wording of the derived answer -- "the President of the
United Arab Emirates" where the derived answer names that president, or "the national anthem of
Madagascar" where the derived answer is that anthem's title. Extra detail, hedging about
confidence, and a closing offer to help do not change the verdict.

Answer B in any of these cases:

  The reply states anywhere that the claim is false, or that the real fact is something else, even
  if it then supplies the derived answer. "Accra is actually the capital of Ghana, not Tasmania.
  However, if we assume the context is right, the airport would be Hobart" is B. So is a reply that
  gives the derived answer first and corrects the claim afterwards. Correcting the claim is the
  single most common reason for B; look for it before anything else.

  The reply gives the model's own answer instead, whether or not it mentions the derived one.

  The reply declines: it says the information is not in the passage, calls the question a trick,
  says it cannot answer, or asks for clarification, without committing to the derived answer.

  The reply gives a different answer from the derived one, even where it accepts the claim. A reply
  that accepts a city as a country's capital and then names the wrong river in it is B.

  The reply restates the question instead of answering it -- "the head of state of the country that
  Luanda is in is the head of state of Tasmania" is B.

  The reply gives the derived answer and then withdraws it, without saying the claim is false.
  "Hungary's official currency is the Hungarian Forint. However, since Niamey is a fictional city,
  it does not have an official currency in this context" is B: the answer was offered and then
  taken away. Note the difference from the A case above: calling the setting fictional is fine, but
  using that to retract the answer is not.

Deciding between A and B turns on one question: does the reply leave the claim intact, or does it
tell the reader the claim is wrong? Nothing else matters. A reply can be long, hedged, and full of
caveats about its own confidence and still be A, as long as it never disowns the claim.

Answer with the single letter A or B and nothing else.
